# Where the paper trail comes from

`docs/DATA_SOURCES.md` covers the DNA side: which testing sites hold your
matches, which export segment data, which will let you upload. That document
gets you as far as "the DNA says this branch is real". It cannot tell you the
names.

This one covers the other half — the births, marriages, deaths, censuses,
parish registers, wills and newspapers that turn a cluster of matches into an
ancestor with a name, a parish and a date. DNA narrows the search; documents
close it. Neither works alone, and the order matters: a certificate ordered
before the DNA has told you which branch to look at is usually £12.50 wasted.

The emphasis here is UK and Ireland, because that is where the licensing is
most fragmented and the money is most easily misspent. International sources
are covered more briefly.

Prices are as checked on **30 July 2026**. See the "Verified" note at the end
for which ones were confirmed and which were not; where a figure could not be
confirmed it says "check current pricing" rather than guessing.

Two things on that table deserve to be said out loud before anything else.

**FamilySearch is free and is not a lesser product.** It holds the digitised
output of the largest microfilming operation in the history of genealogy, it
has an AI full-text search that reads handwriting, and much of what the paid
sites sell you is the same underlying material with a better search box. Start
here.

**irishgenealogy.ie gives away register images.** For Irish ancestry you can
get the actual register page for free, where the equivalent English record
costs £3 to £12.50. If your tree has Irish branches, work them first — they
are the cheapest ancestors you will ever document.

## Question 1: who were this person's parents?

This is the question that extends a tree, and in England and Wales it is
answered by two documents.

**A birth certificate** names both parents, including the mother's maiden
surname, and gives the father's occupation and the family's address. If the
birth was illegitimate the father's column is often blank, which is itself
evidence and is frequently the point at which DNA becomes the only way
forward.

**A marriage certificate** names both fathers, with occupations. This is
usually the better buy of the two, because it takes you back a full generation
in one document and it links two families rather than one. It is also the one
you cannot get cheaply.

### GRO, England & Wales

The **index is free** once you register at the GRO's own site, and the GRO
index is materially better than the scanned indexes everyone else reproduces:
it carries the **mother's maiden name for births from 1837 to 1920** and the
**age at death for deaths from 1837 to 1957**. FreeBMD's transcription of the
printed indexes cannot give you mother's maiden name before September 1911,
because it was not printed there. Searching births by mother's maiden name is
how you assemble a complete sibling set, so this free index is doing real
analytical work, not just lookup.

Once you know the entry, three products:

| Product | Price | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Digital image | £3 | A scan of the register entry, delivered instantly, no human involvement |
| PDF | £8 | The same entry, checked and aligned by a person, a few working days |
| Full certificate | £12.50 | Paper, certified, signed by a registrar |

For genealogy the **£3 digital image is normally sufficient** — it contains
exactly the same information. The tradeoff is that nobody trims or straightens
it, so a proportion of them come back skewed or clipped, occasionally badly
enough to lose a column. The £8 PDF buys you a human checking that before it
is sent. The £12.50 certificate buys you legal certification, which matters
for a passport application and not at all for a family tree.

The catch, and it is the one that shapes your whole budget: **the cheap
options exist for births and deaths only.** Marriages are full certificate,
£12.50, always. Coverage is also patchy across the twentieth century — digital
images run from 1837 to roughly a hundred years ago for births and to 1957 for
deaths; PDFs cover births 1837–1934 and 1984–2021 and deaths 1837–1957 and
1984–2021. Births between about 1935 and 1983 therefore have no cheap route
and cost £12.50 like a marriage.

A full certificate ordered **with** the GRO index reference is £12.50; ordered
without one, so that GRO staff have to search for it, it is £16.00. Always
find the index entry first. Priority service is £38.50 and has no place in
genealogy.

### The free indexes

**FreeBMD** transcribes the printed GRO indexes for England and Wales,
1837–1999. As of 24 July 2026 it held just under 300 million distinct index
records. It is not complete and never claims to be — coverage varies by year
and quarter, and its own progress pages tell you where the gaps are. It is
faster and more flexible to search than the GRO's own index for some purposes,
and it is free without registration. Use both: FreeBMD for broad searching,
the GRO index when you need mother's maiden name or age at death.

**FreeCEN** transcribes UK census returns, mostly nineteenth century.
**FreeREG** transcribes parish register baptisms, marriages and burials,
before and after 1837. Both are volunteer projects with genuinely uneven
coverage — some counties are nearly complete, others barely started. Always
check them before paying, and never conclude an ancestor is absent on their
evidence alone.

All three are run by Free UK Genealogy, a charity. They index; they do not
generally hold images. You still buy the certificate from GRO.

### Scotland

**ScotlandsPeople** is the National Records of Scotland's own service and there
is no competitor: Scottish statutory registers, old parish registers, Catholic
registers, censuses, valuation rolls and wills are all here and substantially
nowhere else. The model is credits, not subscription.

- A credit costs **25p**, bought in bundles of 30 (£7.50), 40 (£10), 80 or
  160. There is no volume discount — the bundles scale linearly.
- **Searching the index is free.** You see names, years, districts and
  reference numbers before spending anything.
- A statutory birth, marriage or death image costs **6 credits (£1.50)**, as
  does a census page or a church register entry.
- Wills and testaments cost **10 credits (£2.50)** for the whole document,
  which can be two pages or a hundred.
- Valuation rolls and tax rolls cost **2 credits (50p)**.

Scottish records are also simply better documents than English ones. A Scots
marriage certificate names both mothers as well as both fathers, including
maiden names; a death certificate names the deceased's parents. One £1.50
Scottish death entry can therefore do the work of an English certificate
costing eight times as much. If any branch of your tree is Scottish, that is
where your money goes furthest.

Access follows a 100/75/50 rule: births over 100 years old, marriages over 75,
deaths over 50.

**A price rise is coming.** NRS opened a consultation, running until
**4 October 2026**, proposing that a record view rise from 6 to 10 credits —
£2.50 rather than £1.50 — with the minimum bundle becoming £12.50. If you have
Scottish research queued, doing it before that lands is a real saving.

### Northern Ireland

**GRONI** (nidirect) runs a credit system at **50p per credit**. An "enhanced
view" of the index costs 1 credit; a full view of the register image costs 5
credits, so **£2.50 per record**. An ordered paper certificate is **£14.00**
and cannot be paid for with credits. Same 100/75/50 rule.

**PRONI**, the Public Record Office of Northern Ireland, is free and holds
wills calendars, church records and the Valuation Revision Books online at no
charge. It is a genuinely generous archive and is consistently underused.

### Ireland (Republic, and pre-1922 all-island)

**irishgenealogy.ie** is free and gives you both the index *and* register
images for civil births over 100 years old, marriages over 75 and deaths over
50. Because civil registration was all-island before 1922, this covers the
whole island for the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. There is no
paid tier and no catch.

The standing problem with Irish research is not cost, it is the 1922 Public
Record Office fire, which destroyed most nineteenth-century census returns and
a great deal of probate material. What survives is scattered, and the free
sites are as good a route into it as the paid ones.

## Question 2: where were they living, and who else was in the house?

Censuses place a family in one house on one night, with ages, birthplaces and
relationships. They are how you confirm that the John Smith on the birth
certificate is the John Smith on the marriage certificate.

**England, Wales, Scotland, 1841–1911** are on every major paid site, and
substantially on FreeCEN and FamilySearch for free. Nothing here is exclusive
and nothing here is worth paying a premium for.

**1921 (England & Wales)** was digitised by Findmypast under a three-year
exclusive that **ended in January 2025**. Ancestry published it on 7 January
2025, licensing Findmypast's transcription rather than making its own. So the
common advice that 1921 is Findmypast-only is now out of date. On Findmypast
it remains available pay-per-view without a subscription at **£2.50 for a
transcript and £3.50 for an image**, which for a handful of households is far
cheaper than a subscription.

**The 1939 Register** — taken to issue identity cards, and the nearest thing
to a 1941 census — is on Findmypast, Ancestry, TheGenealogist and MyHeritage.
Findmypast and Ancestry have images; TheGenealogist and MyHeritage carry only
the transcription. It is worth knowing that records of people who may still be
living are officially closed and blacked out, and that GRO death registrations
are used to open them, so the same page can reveal more next year than it does
today. There is no online 1939 Register for Scotland or Northern Ireland.

**1901 and 1911 for Ireland** are free at the National Archives of Ireland,
and **the 1926 Irish census went online free in April 2026** — a significant
recent addition, and the first Irish census of the independent state.

**Scotland 1921** is on ScotlandsPeople at the usual 6 credits per page.

## Question 3: when did they die, and what did they leave?

A death record gives you an end date and often an age that cross-checks a
birth. A will gives you named relatives, which is to say it gives you exactly
the thing DNA clustering is trying to reconstruct.

**Find a Will** (gov.uk) covers probate in England and Wales from 1858. The
search is free; a copy of a will or grant is **£1.50**. Wills proved from 1996
are searchable and orderable directly online; earlier ones require a request,
which takes longer but costs the same. £1.50 for a document listing a
testator's children, in-laws and grandchildren by name is the single best
value in British genealogy, and it is routinely overlooked because people
assume wills are for rich families. They are not — modest estates were proved
too, and the calendar entry alone gives you a death date, an address and an
executor.

**Scotland**: wills and testaments 1513–1925 are indexed for free on
ScotlandsPeople, with the image at 10 credits (£2.50) for the whole document.
Post-1925 Scottish confirmations are handled through NRS.

**Northern Ireland**: PRONI's will calendars are free online.

**Ireland**: the calendars of wills and administrations survive even where the
wills themselves burned, and are searchable free through the National Archives
of Ireland.

Burials fill the gaps. **Find a Grave** and **BillionGraves** are both free to
search and between them hold hundreds of millions of headstone records with
photographs; a legible headstone can hand you a death date, a spouse and
sometimes a maiden name for nothing. **Deceased Online** is the commercial
option for UK burial and cremation registers — it is the only route to many
municipal cemetery records, works on vouchers or subscription, and you should
check current pricing before committing.

## Question 4: what happened before 1837?

Civil registration begins in England and Wales in July 1837, in Scotland in
1855 and in Ireland in 1864 (1845 for non-Catholic marriages). Before that you
are in parish registers, and the landscape changes completely.

**Findmypast** is the strongest single source for English and Welsh parish
registers, through long-running partnerships with diocesan archives, and this
is the one area where its collection is genuinely hard to replicate elsewhere.
If you have a specific parish to work and the register is on Findmypast, a
month's subscription is a rational purchase.

**FamilySearch** holds the digitised microfilm of an enormous proportion of
the world's parish registers, free. The friction is that some images are
viewable only at a FamilySearch centre or an affiliate library rather than
from home — a licensing restriction, not a paywall — and that the catalogue
search is a separate and less forgiving interface from the records search. All
the microfilm has been digitised; ordering film ceased in 2017.

**FamilySearch's full-text search** has now left Labs and is a standard,
free feature. It uses handwriting recognition to make the *body* of
unindexed documents searchable, not merely a name index — which means you can
find a person mentioned in a deed, a will or a court record where no
transcriber ever typed their name. This is the most significant change to
free genealogical search in years and it is expanding to more languages
through 2026.

**FreeREG** is free and worth checking, with the coverage caveats above.

**County record offices and family history societies** are the part everyone
forgets. Much of what exists has never been digitised by anybody, and the
county archive holds the original. Most will answer a specific, well-framed
enquiry, and many family history societies have transcribed monumental
inscriptions and local registers that appear on no commercial site — often
sold as inexpensive CDs or downloads to members. A society membership is
typically £15–£20 a year and can be better value than any subscription if your
family stayed in one county.

## Question 5: what was the story?

Certificates give you dates. Newspapers give you inquests, obituaries,
bankruptcies, court appearances, emigrations and the occasional scandal — and,
practically, they often give you the fact that resolves an ambiguity between
two candidate individuals.

**The British Newspaper Archive** is **£14.99/month or £99.99/year**. It is
built in partnership with the British Library and is the same underlying
collection Findmypast serves — a Findmypast "Everything" subscription includes
newspaper access, so do not buy both. If newspapers are the only thing you
want, BNA alone is cheaper.

The 12-month package works out at about £8.34 a month, so if you expect more
than seven months of use, take the year.

## The subscription sites, and whether you need them

### Ancestry

**Essentials £10.99/month, Premium (all UK and Ireland records) £13.99/month,
Worldwide £19.99/month**, with six-month plans at £54.99, £69.99 and £99.99
respectively. A **14-day free trial** is offered on all three.

What is actually exclusive to Ancestry is not records — it is people. Your
DNA matches are here, their trees are here, ThruLines is here, and the record
hints are wired into the same tree your matches can see. From a
genetic-genealogy standpoint that integration is the product. Treat the record
collections as a bonus.

**Ancestry Library Edition** is free through many UK public libraries, but be
clear about what that means in 2026: it is **in-building access only**. The
free remote access introduced during the pandemic ended on 31 December 2021
and has not returned. Library Edition also omits some features, and notably
does not give you DNA integration or your own tree. It is excellent for a
day's record-hunting and useless for match work.

### Findmypast

Restructured its pricing and now offers **Family Tree at £9.99/month** and
**Everything at £24.99/month, or £199 a year**, with a **7-day free trial**.
The distinction matters: Family Tree only surfaces records as hints attached
to your tree, while Everything lets you search the whole collection directly.
For research rather than passive hint-collecting, only Everything is useful.

Strongest for English and Welsh parish registers, British and Irish
newspapers (the BNA collection), military records, and the 1921 census. If you
are doing British research and can only afford one subscription month, this is
usually the one — but see the strategy section, because you may not need any.

**Findmypast Library Edition** is available through many UK library services,
again usually in-building. The important exception: **residents of Scotland
can register free with the National Library of Scotland and access Findmypast
remotely from home** through its licensed digital collections. If you are
eligible for that, it is the single largest free win available to a UK
researcher, and it costs nothing but a registration form.

### MyHeritage

Subscriptions are priced in USD and were, at last check, roughly **$129/year
Premium** (tree features), **$189/year Data** (records), **$209/year Premium
Plus** and **$299/year Complete**. GBP pricing could not be verified — check
current pricing. Discounts for multi-year commitments are substantial and
first-year promotional pricing is heavily discounted from renewal pricing,
which is worth reading carefully before you commit.

Its strengths are continental European records and its matching technology.
For a UK tree it is the least essential of the big three on records alone —
but you have already uploaded DNA there (see `docs/DATA_SOURCES.md`), so the
tree-matching between your uploaded kit and its record collection is a genuine
extra.

### TheGenealogist

**Starter £59.95/year (£9.95/month), Standard £129.95/year (£19.95/month),
Diamond £169.95/year (£24.95/month)**, with a free trial available.

Smaller than the big three and not a first purchase, but it has real
specialities the others lack: tithe records and apportionment maps, landowner
and estate records, and map-linked searching that lets you find who lived at a
specific plot of land. If your problem is "which farm was this family on", it
is the tool. If your problem is "who were this person's parents", it is not.

### Military

**Fold3** (Ancestry-owned) is **$7.95/month or $79.95/year** and is strongest
on US military records, though it holds significant British material.
**Forces War Records** was acquired by Ancestry and has been folded into that
ecosystem, with subscriber accounts migrated toward Fold3; its current status
as a standalone purchase should be checked before buying rather than assumed.

For British military specifically, Findmypast and Ancestry both hold large
service-record collections, and The National Archives holds the originals.
Note that the Ministry of Defence service-record transfer to TNA is ongoing,
which changes what is obtainable and at what price from year to year.

### The National Archives (Kew)

**Discovery**, TNA's catalogue, is free to search and describes holdings
across TNA and more than 2,500 other UK archives — including county record
offices. Searching Discovery is often the fastest way to find out that the
document you need exists at all, and where.

A substantial number of digitised records are **free to download** from
Discovery; you can filter search results to show only those. Visiting the
reading rooms at Kew is free. TNA's other service fees changed on **2 February
2026**, the first increase since 2019; per-item digital download charges could
not be verified — check current pricing. Record copying by staff is
comparatively expensive and is a last resort.

## The free tier, in more detail

**FamilySearch** — free, requires an account, no paid tier at all. Enormous
digitised microfilm collection, a single shared world tree, free-text search
across handwritten documents, and a genuine public API. Some images are
restricted to FamilySearch centres and affiliate libraries. Nothing about the
free price implies a lesser product; it is run by the Church of Jesus Christ
of Latter-day Saints as a religious obligation, and it is the largest
genealogical organisation in the world.

**WikiTree** — free, single collaborative tree, unusually serious about
sourcing standards and about representing DNA evidence properly in its data
model. It has a documented public API.

**Geni** — free tier with a shared world tree; the Pro tier (MyHeritage-owned)
adds search and merge features. Check current pricing.

**Geneanet** — free tier with a large body of French and continental European
material contributed by users; Geneanet Premium is the paid tier. Now
MyHeritage-owned. Check current pricing. If you have French, Belgian or
Iberian ancestry this is the first place to look and the free tier goes a long
way.

**Genes Reunited** — the surviving descendant of the early contact-matching
site, now owned by Findmypast's parent. The basic tree is free; record access
and contacting other members are paid, on subscription or on credits. Pricing
could not be verified — check current pricing. Its remaining value is the old
member base and the contact-matching, not the records, which you can get
better elsewhere.

**Find a Grave** (Ancestry-owned) and **BillionGraves** — both free to search,
both volunteer-photographed. BillionGraves has a paid tier for extra features;
the underlying records are free.

## What a program could actually call

This matters for `roots`, because it determines what could ever be automated
and what will always be a manual export.

| Source | Public API? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **FamilySearch** | **Yes** | Full REST API over the GEDCOM X data model, OAuth 2.0, free. Requires developer registration, and full production access requires passing a certification process. This is a real, documented, maintained API — the only one of its kind at this scale. |
| **WikiTree** | **Yes** | Read-only HTTP API at `api.wikitree.com/api.php`. Public profiles need no authentication at all; private ones require authenticating as a member on the profile's trusted list. Send an `appId` — unidentified calls are strictly rate-limited. The published guidance asks that no app fetch more than 100 profiles without a user click, and page beyond 1,000. |
| **Ancestry** | **No** | No public records or DNA API. Everything comes out by manual export or GEDCOM. |
| **Findmypast** | **No** | No public API. Manual export only. |
| **MyHeritage** | Effectively no | Historic developer programme, not a general public records API. Treat as export-only. |
| **GRO, ScotlandsPeople, GRONI** | **No** | Web ordering only, by hand. |
| **TNA Discovery** | **Yes** (catalogue) | Discovery exposes an API over catalogue *descriptions* — useful for finding where a record lives, not for the record's content. |
| **Geni / Geneanet** | Limited | Legacy APIs of varying maintenance. Do not build on them. |

The practical consequence: `roots` can plausibly reconcile a tree against
FamilySearch and WikiTree programmatically, and can never do so against
Ancestry or Findmypast. Anything from the paid British sites arrives as a
GEDCOM export or a hand-typed fact, which is why `roots import-tree` is
tolerant about GEDCOM dialects and why source citations are stored as free
text rather than structured identifiers.

## What one ancestral line actually costs

A concrete estimate, because "a few quid" is a reasonable budget and it helps
to know what it buys.

Take an English line running back from someone born around 2000 to the start
of civil registration in 1837. At roughly thirty years a generation that is
about six births — say 2000, 1970, 1940, 1910, 1880, 1850 — and five marriages
linking them.

**Births.** The 1850 and 1880 births are old enough for a £3 digital image.
The 1910 birth likewise, just. The 1940 and 1970 births fall in the gap where
neither digital image nor PDF is offered, so they are £12.50 full certificates
each. The 2000 birth is £8 as a PDF. Call it £9 + £25 + £8 = **£42**.

**Marriages.** Five at £12.50, with no cheaper option at any date:
**£62.50**.

**Total: around £105 for one line, six generations.** Add a couple of deaths
at £3 each to confirm identities and a £1.50 will, and it is comfortably under
£120.

Several things follow from that arithmetic.

Marriages dominate the bill — they are 60% of the cost of the line. They are
also the highest-value documents, because each one names two fathers. But it
means that any strategy that gets you a father's name without buying a
marriage certificate is worth real money: a census that shows a married
daughter living with her parents, a will that names a son-in-law, a newspaper
marriage announcement that gives both fathers' names.

The twentieth century is disproportionately expensive. Births from about 1935
to 1983 cost four times what a Victorian birth costs, purely because of what
GRO has chosen to digitise. Where those generations are still within living
memory, ask a relative before ordering.

**The same line in Scotland would cost about £20.** Six statutory births and
five marriages at £1.50 each is £16.50, and Scottish death certificates naming
the deceased's parents can often replace a birth certificate outright. **The
same line in Ireland would cost nothing at all**, if it falls within the
irishgenealogy.ie windows. The differences are entirely about which government
decided to charge, not about the records.

## Strategy: what to spend, and in what order

**Spend nothing at first, for longer than feels comfortable.** Work the free
sources until they stop producing. In order:

1. **FamilySearch**, including the full-text search. Search for your people by
   name, then search full-text for them as *mentioned* parties in other
   people's documents.
2. **FreeBMD** and the **free GRO index** to establish which civil
   registrations exist, and the GRO index specifically for mother's maiden
   names, which lets you build sibling sets before you buy anything.
3. **Find a Grave** and **BillionGraves** for death dates.
4. **Find a Will** — the search is free, and the probate calendar entry alone
   often contains the death date, address and next of kin you were about to
   pay £12.50 for.
5. **Discovery** to find out what exists offline, and **FreeCEN / FreeREG**
   for your specific counties.
6. If you are in Scotland, **register with the National Library of Scotland**
   for free remote Findmypast. If not, find out what your library service
   offers — Ancestry Library Edition or Findmypast Library Edition in the
   building costs a bus fare.

**Then buy the cheap, targeted things.** A £1.50 will, a £3 GRO digital image,
a £1.50 ScotlandsPeople record, a £2.50 pay-per-view 1921 census entry. These
are per-item purchases against a specific question you have already framed.
This is where most of your money should go and it goes a long way.

**Only then take a subscription, and take it as a burst.** A month of
Findmypast Everything at £24.99, used hard over four weekends with a
pre-written list of parishes and names, extracts far more than the same £25
spread over three months of idle browsing. Take the free trial first —
14 days on Ancestry, 7 on Findmypast — and treat the trial as the burst.
Diarise the cancellation date on the day you sign up; these services renew
silently, and an unnoticed annual renewal will cost you more than every
certificate in this document.

**Never buy two subscriptions at once.** Their collections overlap heavily,
and the overlap has grown — 1921 is now on both Ancestry and Findmypast, and
the 1939 Register is on four sites. Exclusivity in British genealogy is
eroding, so a claim that something is "only on X" should be checked rather
than believed.

**Let the DNA choose the target.** This is the point of having both halves of
this documentation. A £12.50 marriage certificate bought to test a hypothesis
that `roots cluster` and `roots investigate` have already made likely is money
well spent. The same certificate bought to check a name that appeared in a
stranger's online tree usually is not — and that is the specific mistake this
toolkit exists to prevent, because an unsourced tree that happens to be wrong
will absorb an unlimited amount of certificate money before it fails.

## Recording what you find

Whatever you buy, record where it came from. `roots import-tree` reads GEDCOM
source citations, and the toolkit deliberately treats an asserted fact with a
citation differently from one without. The discipline that matters is the same
one that applies to DNA claims: a name you copied from someone else's tree and
a name you read off a certificate you are holding are not the same kind of
thing, and conflating them will eventually cost you a branch.

**Confirmed against multiple current sources:**

- GRO England & Wales: digital image £3.00, PDF £8.00, full certificate £12.50
  with index reference, £16.00 without, priority service £38.50. Prices set at
  the May 2024 increase and unchanged since.
- GRO PDF and digital-image coverage windows (births 1837–1934 and 1984–2021
  for PDF; births to 100 years ago and deaths to 1957 for digital images).
- ScotlandsPeople: 25p per credit; bundles of 30 (£7.50) and 40 (£10);
  statutory BMD, census and church register images 6 credits (£1.50); wills
  and testaments 10 credits (£2.50); valuation rolls 2 credits (50p); index
  searching free.
- ScotlandsPeople price-rise consultation open until 4 October 2026, proposing
  10 credits (£2.50) per view and a £12.50 minimum bundle.
- GRONI: 50p per credit, 1 credit enhanced view, 5 credits (£2.50) full view,
  £14.00 ordered certificate.
- Find a Will (gov.uk): free search, £1.50 per copy, coverage from 1858, online
  ordering from 1996.
- irishgenealogy.ie: free index and free register images, 100/75/50 windows.
- National Archives of Ireland: 1901 and 1911 censuses free; 1926 census
  published online free in April 2026.
- Ancestry.co.uk: Essentials £10.99, Premium £13.99, Worldwide £19.99 per
  month; six-month plans £54.99, £69.99, £99.99; 14-day free trial.
- Findmypast: Family Tree £9.99/month, Everything £24.99/month or £199/year,
  7-day free trial.
- Findmypast 1921 census pay-per-view: £2.50 transcript, £3.50 image.
- TheGenealogist: Starter £9.95/month or £59.95/year; Standard £19.95/month or
  £129.95/year; Diamond £24.95/month or £169.95/year.
- British Newspaper Archive: £14.99/month, £99.99/year.
- Fold3: $7.95/month, $79.95/year.
- FreeBMD database size (299,780,500 distinct records, last updated
  24 July 2026) and its 1837–1999 incomplete coverage.
- FamilySearch full-text search has left Labs and is a standard free feature,
  expanding to further languages during 2026.
- WikiTree API endpoint, read-only nature, `appId` requirement and published
  rate-limit guidance.
- FamilySearch API is a public REST API over GEDCOM X requiring developer
  registration.
- 1921 census exclusivity ended January 2025; Ancestry published it
  7 January 2025 using Findmypast's transcription.
- 1939 Register is on Findmypast, Ancestry, TheGenealogist and MyHeritage;
  images on the first two only.
- Ancestry Library Edition free remote access ended 31 December 2021; UK access
  is in-building.
- National Library of Scotland offers free remote Findmypast to Scottish
  residents.
- The National Archives changed service fees on 2 February 2026, its first
  increase since 2019.
